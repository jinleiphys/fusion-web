A cavity has a fixed gap spacing, matched to one velocity βopt: an ion that is too slow or too fast arrives at the second gap with the field already turned. So the linac is a relay of four cavity families, each used where T(β) is high. The energy gained per nucleon is (Q/A) times the voltage: a uranium ion carrying 33 charges gains 2.4 times less than one carrying 78, which is why the beam is stripped at 17 MeV/u.

The ion leaves the source at 12 keV/u. Uranium then moves at β = 0.005, and half an RF period at 80.5 MHz is a flight of under 1 cm: no room for drift tubes with focusing magnets. FRIB's first accelerator is therefore a radio-frequency quadrupole, which bunches, focuses and accelerates with shaped electrode vanes and has no drift tubes, from 12 keV/u to 0.5 MeV/u. Once the ions are faster, the frequency can be raised fourfold, to 322 MHz, for the half-wave resonators.

How it is computed

two gaps, βoptλ/2 apart, opposite fields: T(β) = |sin(πβopt/(2β))|
gain at crest per nucleon: ΔW/A = (Q/A) Veff T(β)/T(βopt)
cell length L = βλ/2, λ = c/f

Thin gaps; the finite-gap factor sin(πg/βλ)/(πg/βλ) is left out, and the real FRIB cavities differ in detail from an ideal two-gap structure. The gain is shown at the crest: the linac runs at a negative synchronous phase φs for longitudinal focusing, which multiplies it by cos φs (FRIB's phase is not quoted here). Veff is the effective voltage at βopt from FRIB's resonator table.

Mean charge after a stripperQ vs E/A
Baron, equilibrium (carbon)

Passing through matter, an ion loses the electrons slower than itself and keeps the faster ones, so the charge after a stripper grows with velocity. FRIB strips uranium at 17 MeV/u in a flowing liquid-lithium film (a solid foil would not survive the beam power) and then accelerates five charge states, 76+ to 80+, at once: a single state would throw away most of the beam. They travel together because the cavities and solenoids act on Q/A within a narrow band, here ±Δq/q in rigidity.

How it is computed, and the sources

Q̄/Z = 1 − exp(−83.275 β/Z0.447)
Qave = Q̄ (1 − exp(−12.905 + 0.2124 Z − 0.00122 Z²))
d = √(Q̄ (0.07535 + 0.19 Y − 0.2654 Y²)), Y = Q̄/Z
P(q) ∝ exp(−(q − Qave)²/(2d²))

Baron's empirical fit to equilibrium charge states behind carbon foils (Baron et al., Nucl. Instrum. Methods A 328, 177 (1993)), as printed in Z. He et al., arXiv:1611.04637, Eq. (30). For uranium at 17 MeV/u it gives a mean of ; FRIB's design value is an average of 78+ with about 85% in 76+ to 80+ [17]. The fit is used where its Gaussian makes sense: width d at least half a charge unit and Qave + 3d ≤ Z. Near full stripping the width polynomial falls to zero (at Y = 0.99995) and the distribution is set by the last one or two K electrons, which the fit does not describe; there the panel shows the mean only, and the curve of Qave against energy is dashed. For light ions (protons, α) at these energies this is the whole range. The fit describes an equilibrium foil; FRIB's lithium film is thinner than equilibrium for ions heavier than argon [21], and measured at 20 MeV/u it gives 73.7 against 76.9 for 1 mg/cm² of carbon [20].

Matrices multiplied numerically, (β, α, γ) propagated through the cell; the analytic thin-lens values are printed beside the numerical ones as a check. The rays start on one invariant, ε = 10 mm mrad, at equally spaced phases. The solenoid rotates the beam as it focuses; the rays are drawn in the frame that rotates with it. FRIB's solenoids reach 8 T, and those of the half-wave cryomodules are 0.5 m long [17]; the cell length here is a free teaching parameter, not FRIB's lattice. The solenoid must fit in its cell, so ℓs is held at or below 0.95 Lc (the slider moves back when Lc shrinks); the focal length and the matrix use the same ℓs, so the field inside the magnet is the B set. Stability is read from the cell's own matrix, |Tr M| < 2; the thin-lens ratios are printed only beside it. Space charge, dispersion and chromaticity are left out.

ARIS, FRIB's fragment separator, takes up to 8 T m within ±5% in momentum and ±40 mrad in angle[19], in three stages: a pre-separator that removes the unreacted beam, then two stages with wedge degraders. Here one Bρ–ΔE–Bρ stage is modelled. The primary beam is the ion chosen on the left, at its energy. Its fragments fly on at nearly the beam velocity, so the first dipole stage selects Bρ ∝ (A/Z)βγ: a whole diagonal of nuclei with the same A/Z survives. A degrader then takes energy ∝ Z²/v² from each, so the second stage, set for the wanted nucleus, rejects neighbours with a different Z.

fragment: γf = γbeam, Q = Z ⇒ Bρ1
degrader: dE/dx (Bethe, Bloch term, effective charge) integrated through t = (d/R)·R(wanted)
keep |Bρ/Bρref − 1| < Δp/p at both stages

K = 0.307075 MeV cm²/mol, y = zα/β, z = Z[1 − exp(−0.95 v/(v0Z2/3))]

Checked against NIST PSTAR and ASTAR in Be and Al and against measured Ar, Kr and Xe stopping (IAEA stopping database): . The shortfall for heavy ions is the missing Lindhard–Sørensen and Barkas terms, so for Xe-like fragments the wedge loss is a few per cent low and Bρ2 1 to 3% high. Because every fragment is shifted alike, it hardly moves the selection relative to the wanted ion, but it does move the absolute setting. So the panel shows which neighbours a stage tuned on the wanted ion lets through; the Bρ2 it prints is good to a few per cent, coarser than the acceptance, and a real separator is set on the measured beam.

Left out, on purpose: production cross sections (the chart says which nuclides pass, not how many arrive), the momentum spread of the fragments (comparable to the acceptance in reality, so real purity is worse), energy loss in the production target, angular acceptance, charge states other than bare, energy-loss straggling, and the wedge shape (a flat slab at the dispersive focus; the wedge angle restores achromatism without changing the mean selection). Nuclides with T1/2 < 100 ns are dropped as they decay before the focal plane.

Magnetic rigidityBρ (T m) vs E/A (MeV)
this ion, charge Qsame ion baremachine limits

A dipole of field B bends a particle of momentum p and charge q on a circle of radius ρ = p/(qB). A ring or a separator is built for a maximum Bρ; an ion fits if its rigidity is below it. Stripping more electrons (larger Q) lowers Bρ in proportion, which is why rings take ions after a stripper and why the bare-ion curve lies lowest.

How it is computed

Matom = Z M(1H) + N mn − A (B/A)
Mion = Matom − Q me (+ Be, neglected)
T = A·(E/A), γ = 1 + T/Mionc², βγ = √(T(T + 2M c²))/(M c²)
p c = βγ M c², Bρ [T m] = p c [MeV] / (299.792458 Q)
Eρ = p v/q [MV]

B/A from NUBASE2020 (Kondev et al., Chin. Phys. C 45, 030001 (2021)), which uses the atomic-mass convention B = Z M(1H) + N mn − Matom. CODATA 2018 constants. The removed electrons' binding Be is neglected: 0.76 MeV for bare uranium, 3×10−6 of the mass. E/A is kinetic energy per nucleon (mass number A); per atomic mass unit would differ by M/(Au) − 1, 2.1×10−4 for the 238U atom (1.4×10−4 for the U33+ ion).

Checked against an independent 30-digit calculation that takes atomic masses from the AME2020 mass table instead of NUBASE: largest relative difference in Bρ .

In a uniform field the revolution frequency qB/(2πγm) falls as the ion speeds up, so it drifts out of step with the fixed RF. An isochronous cyclotron raises the average field with radius exactly as γ(r) grows; sectors (hills and valleys) restore the vertical focusing that a rising field takes away.

How it is computed

frev = Q e B / (2π γ M), fRF = h frev; isochronous B = B0γ ⇒ frev = Q e B0/(2π M) on every orbit
v = ω r ⇒ β(r) = r/r∞, r∞ = c/ω0 = Mc/(Q e B0)
B(r) = B0 γ(r) = B0/√(1 − r²/r∞²)
bending limit E/A = KB (Q/A)², KB = e²(Bρ)ext²/(2u) = 48.24 (Bρ)² MeV (non-relativistic)
focusing limit E/A = KF (Q/A)
relativistic: Bρ = (A/Q) √(Tn(Tn + 2Mc²/A))/c = Bρnr √(1 + Tn/(2Mc²/A))

KB is the bending limit, defined through the kinetic energy E/A = KB(Q/A)² [6]. Read literally as a rigidity it only holds non-relativistically: at fixed KB the rigidity actually needed grows by √(1 + Tn/(2Mc²/A)), 9% at 345 MeV/u. Light ions are held lower by the vertical focusing, E/A ≤ KF(Q/A) in its conventional form; KF is taken from the review [6] where it is tabulated (compact superconducting cyclotrons), and where it is not, only the bending limit is shown and the panel says so. Both are design estimates: AGOR's limits give 220 MeV for protons, it delivered 190 MeV [6].

Example: RIBF's SRC, K = 2600 MeV, gives for very heavy ions [7].

FRIB's experimental areasclick one in the scene

After ARIS the rare-isotope beam goes three ways: fast beams at near production energy to the south vaults and the HRS hall; beams stopped in gas in N4 for precision work; and stopped beams reaccelerated in ReA. The arrangement follows the published layout; positions and room sizes in the scene are schematic, the instruments generic shapes at roughly their size.

S1
FRIB Decay Station initiator (FDSi): implantation detector surrounded by germanium detectors, for β decay of fast-beam fragments. [a]
S2
Sweeper magnet and the MoNA-LISA neutron array (walls of plastic-scintillator bars): unbound states seen through their neutrons. [a]
S3
S800 spectrograph: superconducting, high resolution, bends vertically. The spectrograph takes up to 4 Tm with a momentum acceptance of about ±3 % and 20 msr; its analysis line up to about 4.9 Tm, within ±0.5 % (dispersion-matched mode) or ±2 % (focused mode). GRETINA ran here in FRIB's first S800 experiment (2022). [b, d, h]
N4
Gas stopping: fast fragments slowed and stopped in helium gas (linear gas cell, cyclotron stopper, cryogenic gas stopper) and extracted at about 60 keV. [b]
stopped beams
LEBIT: Penning-trap mass measurements in a 9.4 T magnet. BECOLA: collinear laser spectroscopy. [b]
ReA3
Reaccelerated beams at astrophysical energies: SECAR (a recoil separator about 40 m long) on its own beam line, with the JENSA gas-jet target at its entrance as its main target; the AT-TPC active target. [b, c, j, k]
ReA6
SOLARIS solenoidal spectrometer (up to 4 T) on its own beam line. GRETA, the γ-ray tracking array, is to serve experiments at ReA and at the S800, where it uses a modified GRETINA frame; the scene shows the fall 2025 configuration, with its frame in this vault. [c, e, i, j]
HRS
High Rigidity Spectrometer (Bρ up to 8 Tm), in two segments: the high-transmission beam line (HRS-HTBL) from ARIS to the reaction target, and the spectrometer section (HRS-SPS). In February 2025 DOE approved CD-2/3 and the start of execution for HRS-HTBL; the instrument as a whole is still under development, so the magnets in the scene are a planned layout, drawn schematically.
