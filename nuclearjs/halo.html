Halo nuclei

A nucleon bound by a few hundred keV spends most of its time outside the nucleus. Drag the binding toward zero: an s-wave cloud swells without limit, a p wave more slowly, a d wave hardly at all, and a proton, held in by the Coulomb barrier, approaches a finite size.

Why only s and p waves

Outside the well a neutron's radial function is u(r) = b·W(2κr), κ = √(2μS)/ħ, with W = e−κr(1 + 1/κr) for l = 1 and e−κr(1 + 3/κr + 3/κ²r²) for l = 2. As S → 0 it tends to the zero-energy solution, which outside the well falls like r−l.

l = 0: u is flat outside, its norm diverges, so the tail carries all the probability: ⟨r²⟩ → 1/(2κ²) = ħ²/(4μS). The rms radius grows like S−1/2.

l = 1: u ~ A/r is normalizable but ∫r²u² is not: b → Aκ and ⟨r²⟩ → (5/4)A²/κ, so the rms radius grows like S−1/4.

l ≥ 2: u ~ A/rl, ∫r²u² converges: the radius stays finite at threshold.

protons: the Coulomb barrier makes the zero-energy tail fall faster than any power, and every l stays finite. This is why proton halos are rare.

All four laws are checked on the live solver down to S = 10−4 MeV (slopes of ln⟨r²⟩ against ln S: −0.991, −0.498, −0.004, −0.000; the rms radius has half these, −0.50 and −0.25).

Matter radius from core + orbital

Point nucleons, core of Ac nucleons, relative coordinate r. About the centre of mass the core sits at −r/A and the last nucleon at (Ac/A)r, so

A⟨r²⟩A = Ac(⟨r²⟩c + ⟨r²⟩/A²) + (Ac/A)²⟨r²⟩
⟨r²⟩A = (Ac/A)⟨r²⟩c + (Ac/A²)⟨r²⟩

⟨r²⟩c is the measured matter radius of the core; ⟨r²⟩ is the orbital computed here. The halo enters damped by Ac/A², yet one nucleon at 7 fm still lifts the matter radius of 11Be about 0.7 fm above that of its 10Be core.

Two-neutron halos

11Li (S2n = MeV) is the classic halo of Tanihata et al. (1985), but it is Borromean: 9Li + n and n + n are both unbound, only the three together bind. A core + one nucleon well cannot describe it; it needs a three-body calculation and is left out here on purpose.
